What it adds up to, roughly

The plan's own measurements, taken on every deploy, run through a few stated assumptions. A sense of scale, not a pro forma.

3.2Msquare feet of floor space, about 3.5× the old mall's 935,000 (54% occupied in 2020)
2,300homes, if the upper floors are housing: about 5,300 people. All of downtown Norfolk had 4,691 in 2023.
740shop fronts along 4.1 km of street, 699 of them P87's own small doors
2,229trees, and a meadow on the open ground. No parking lots.

How many ground floors can the people upstairs carry?

740 shop fronts for about 5,300 people is about 140 for every 1,000. Paris has 28 ground-floor shops, cafés and services per 1,000 residents, Barcelona 37, and both have tourists and commuters helping. By the block model's own measure, the people upstairs carry about 150 to 200 ground floors, and most of those aren't shops. Of Barcelona's 61,875 ground-floor businesses, 21,167 are shops and 33,740 are services: hairdressers, clinics, agencies, repair, offices at street level. At Barcelona's rates, the people here would carry about 70 shops and 110 services. The rest of the 740 fronts are the bet: pull from the light rail and the region, or work.

What a shop here has to sell

Nothing like this block has been built in Hampton Roads, so the market can't price it. We price the space at what it costs. Shop space costs $205 to $290 a square foot to build (Charlotte, the nearest city in Rider Levett Bucknall's survey), before land and fees. What that comes to in rent depends on who carries the money:

Who carries the moneyRent a sq ft a yearA shop must sell, a year

For contrast, existing shop space in Norfolk asks $14.75 a square foot a year, before running costs. Borrowed at the bank's rate, new space has to ask about $21 before running costs, half again as much, which is why the market hasn't built it. Public money brings it to about $16, about level with old space. Held in common and paid for, a shop's break-even falls by about a quarter. The break-evens are floors: the shopkeeper earns Norfolk's living wage, and utilities, insurance and card fees aren't counted.

The pull gap

If every dollar the people upstairs spend in shops stayed here, about $37M a year, it would carry about 160 shops at that break-even. The other 580 need about $130M a year from outside the site, roughly 2.4× everything downtown's Granby sub-district sold in FY2023. That's on purpose: the plan is a frame for a regional destination, and the gap is the pull it would have to earn.

Against the doom loop

Cushman & Wakefield and Christopher Leinberger studied 208 walkable urban places in 15 US cities, which they call WalkUPs: 3% of those cities' land, 26% of their real estate value, 37% of their tax revenue. The ones that do best mix living, working and playing; downtowns lost value because they were mostly work. Their best mix, by floor area, averages 42% work (anywhere from 24% to 62% can work), 31% living and 26% play. And WalkUPs, they write, are not “bedroom communities, where residential tends to be between 80% to 90% of all inventory.” This plan is 77% living:

The mall was the doom loop's suburban cousin: one use, and when the shopping left, nothing held the place up. Unless some of those upper floors become offices, studios and workshops, the plan trades one monoculture for another, with shops that wait all day for people to come home. In the study, 70% of these places' foot traffic came from people visiting things to do. That's the pull the gap above needs, and shops are only part of it: a market hall, music, a hotel by the station. Some of what the people here need takes more of them, too: a walkable grocery store wants 5,000 to 10,000 homes nearby before it opens, and the plan has about 2,300.

Their other levers, and what each looks like held in common. More homes for sale, not just for rent: here, limited-equity co-ops and land-trust homes, which keep the gains with the people who live there. Place management, which they mean as business improvement districts: here, the neighborhood running its own streets and squares. Faster permits and incentives for reuse: the city's call. And the tax base: at Norfolk's rate, the plan's buildings would pay about $11M a year in real estate tax if assessed at what they cost to build, before counting the land.

The light rail

HRT studied taking the Tide two miles from Newtown Road, past Sentara Leigh, to a last station on this site (an early estimate put it near $225M), then paused the study in August 2023 because the city hadn't settled where that station goes. It was still on hold in July 2025. The plan sketches an answer: a platform 133 m from its streets, at the end of a line from the direction of Newtown Road. It isn't HRT's route. Today the nearest station, Military Highway, is about 1 km away, across I‑264.

How the numbers are built
  • Measured from the plan on every deploy (tools/vibe-render/plan_numbers.py): floor area is each building's footprint times its floors; upper floors are that minus the ground floor; shop fronts are the ones the render opens at street-facing doors.
  • Homes: upper floors × 85% usable ÷ 900 sq ft a home, our assumption (range: 80% and 1,000 sq ft, to 88% and 800). The pipeline assigns no uses; every floor of offices means fewer homes and a bigger lunch crowd. People: 2.27 a household, Norfolk's average.
  • Ground floors per resident: Paris, 60,846 ground-floor shops, bars, restaurants and services, 28 per 1,000 residents (Apur's 2023 census); Barcelona, 61,875 ground-floor businesses, 3.7 per 100 (2024), of which 21,167 are shops and 33,740 services; its residents (about 1.67 million) are implied by those two figures. City-wide figures, with visitors' and commuters' spending in them, so a ceiling for what residents alone carry; Barcelona's census credits its density to “urban compactness and the large number of visitors.”
  • Rent at cost: the middle of RLB's $205–290 hard cost for strip retail in Charlotte (Q3 2026; no land, design or financing fees, so low), paid off as a loan, plus $10.33 a square foot a year to run (IREM's 2024 national figure for offices, taxes included; no public retail figure). A bank loan is the prime rate, 7.00%, over 25 years (our term); public money is the 10-year Treasury rate, 4.85%, over 30 years (our term); already paid for is running costs only.
  • A shop's break-even: rent for 900 sq ft plus Norfolk's living wage for one adult ($47,273, MIT) divided by a 31.1% gross margin, the average for all US retail in 2022 (Census). Margins run from 28% at grocers and 31% at wine shops to 45% at book and hobby stores and 51% at clothing stores. Cafés and restaurants keep about 68% after food and drink, but pay staff: this doesn't model them.
  • Spending: $15,662 a household a year on food at home and out, clothes, alcohol, furnishings, housekeeping, personal care, reading and a quarter of entertainment (BLS, 2023, middle income fifth where published).
  • Live, work, play: the plan's upper floors counted as living and its ground floors as play (shop fronts take about 88% of them; entrances and common rooms the rest). The report's shares are of real estate inventory by square footage. Its optimal mix, found by a random-forest model balancing place-based GDP and value per square foot, averages 42.0% work (90% band 23.8–61.7%), 31.8% living (22.8–46.6%) and 26.2% play (17.0–31.0%); downtowns are 69.7% work, 15.9% living and 14.5% play. It sets aside suburban WalkUPs, “redevelopment of edge cities” among them, which is what this site would be. The grocery threshold is from the same report.
  • Tax: Norfolk's real estate rate, $1.23 per $100 of assessed value, on the middle of RLB's Charlotte hard costs: $248 a square foot for the ground floors (strip retail, $205–290) and $298 for the upper floors (multifamily, $220–375). Assessments needn't match cost; land isn't counted.
  • Not modeled: jobs, what office workers would spend nearby, visitors, competition from nearby stores, and the shopkeeper living upstairs at cost too, which would lower the pay a shop has to cover.
  • The acreage disagrees: the site's perimeter in the data measures 69 acres, the city's RFQ says about 89 with parking and outparcels, and the project page says 97.7. Nothing here is per acre.

Sources: Cushman & Wakefield and Places Platform, Reimagining Cities: Disrupting the Urban Doom Loop · City of Norfolk, real estate tax rate · Ville de Paris / Apur (2023) · Barcelona commercial census (2024) · RLB, North America Quarterly Cost Report (Q3 2026) · Cushman & Wakefield | Thalhimer, Hampton Roads Retail (Q3 2026) · IREM, Income/Expense IQ (2024) · Federal Reserve H.15 (prime rate) · US Treasury (10-year yield) · MIT Living Wage, Norfolk · Census, Annual Retail Trade Survey (2022) · Census QuickFacts, Norfolk · BLS, Consumer Expenditures in 2023 · Norfolk EDA, Military Circle RFQ (2020) · Downtown Norfolk Council, Market Assessment (2023) · HRT, Naval Station Norfolk Transit Corridor · WHRO (2021) · The Virginian-Pilot (July 2025)

How these renders are made

Massing, regenerated from the pipeline on every deploy: polygons extruded with cadquery/OpenCascade, a check on scale and density, not an architectural rendering. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom; it's the same .glb you can download below. P221 places every window and door from each building's own walls and floors. The ground floors' rooms are computed too (P127, P129, P130, P131, P133: public to private, a common area, an entrance room, how rooms connect, a stair core); upper floors have walls and windows only. See the floor plans below, and tools/vibe-render/render.py for the full caveats.

Speculative — not derived from site data

Vision

Everything above this line is computed: real parcels, real pattern math, real citations, honestly labeled gaps. This section is the opposite, on purpose — a place for imagination to run ahead of what the pipeline has actually verified. A mood board, a hand sketch, an AI-illustrated concept of what this could feel like to stand in — dreaming out loud about a place is real, necessary work, especially for a neighborhood that's been told "no" by real systems for a long time. It isn't a lesser kind of contribution than the geometry above.

It will never be merged into the render above, and the render above will never quietly borrow from it. Nothing here is a proposal, a commitment, or a claim about this site — it's a place to imagine together before anything is decided, kept in view, but never confused with what's actually real.

Nothing has been added here yet. This space is reserved for the neighborhood's own vision — sketches, references, AI-illustrated concepts — each one clearly labeled, none of them mistaken for a real proposal.

This tool does not measure your neighborhood. Aliveness is what Alexander called the quality without a name — by definition not fully namable. What you're about to read is a chorus of opinions, each citing its source, encoded in math. The interesting part is where they disagree, or where they tell you what they couldn't see.

What's accurate is the record of what people decide. Everything below is one or another opinion. The geometric voices come from Alexander & Salingaros. The activist voice comes from Jason and the Eastside Commons coalition. Your voice — the one not yet captured here — is the only one that knows whether this place is alive to live in.

The map is one more opinion. Parcels are an administrative cut by Norfolk City GIS — they are not buildings, sidewalks, or the way people walk this place. A neighborhood is more than its polygons. Basemap tiles fetched from openstreetmap.org; if those don't load the parcels still work.
