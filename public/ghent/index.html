This is Ghent, in Norfolk, Virginia: the Ghent Neighborhood League's area, built from the City's own maps. Every ground floor the Ghent Streets proposal would open to a small shop, café or office is open here, in the building that stands there today, behind the doors and windows it has now.

      What's here. The proposal is Jason Edelman's maximal reading of Ghent Streets' amendment: every parcel in the League's area that NFK2050, Norfolk's comprehensive plan, would let hold retail (along arterial and collector streets in its Sustain place type, p. 69; on the medical campus, pp. 80–81; in the Regional Activity Center, p. 82), plus HC-G2's corner lots, plus every lot on Graydon Avenue and Blow Street. Where there's a building to adapt, its ground floor holds one of the uses the amendment would permit by right, at most 3,500 sq ft, in Policy Brief No. 2's mix: half cafés and restaurants, three tenths shops, a fifth services.

      Doors and windows stay where they are. This is adaptive reuse: the buildings are adapted, not remodelled. A house keeps its door and windows, and the new use shows through them, with a sign over the door, awnings, and tables in the yard. Only a building that's already commercial gets a storefront.

      What's whose. The City of Norfolk's open data: the League's boundary, zoning, parcels, building footprints and heights, street centerlines and their class, addresses, assessments, the tree inventory, and the assessor's photos. NFK2050's place types are the City's own map (its ArcGIS layer is titled DRAFT). Data inferred where not provided: roof shapes (a hip or gable on houses that nearly fill a rectangle, flat otherwise), windows and doors (laid out in bays), sidewalks, tree heights and crowns (from trunk diameters), and the few heights the City's data lacks. Porches, yards and walls are grown for each house from how often things appear in 121 of the City assessor's street photos of Ghent: four in five houses have a porch, mostly on round columns behind white balusters; most fronts are open to the sidewalk, with clipped evergreens along the foundation and a small tree in the yard; a third of the houses are brick below and shingle or siding above. What any one house gets is a draw from those odds, not a claim about that house. Ours: which business is where within the brief's mix, their signs and what they put out, and the people. Day to day: walks along the City's street centerlines; today's places from Overture Maps; what has opened from the City's new business licences since 2019; rent from the Census Bureau's American Community Survey (2020–2024), by tract; how often a place is new from the Census Bureau's Business Dynamics Statistics for the Virginia Beach–Norfolk metro. What the people say is quoted word for word from Policy Brief No. 1 (its objections and responses, §IX), the zines, and NFK2050 as the brief quotes it; the log shows each line's source. The streetlights stand where Dominion Energy's records (the City's open data) put them, each by its fixture style and pole: mostly Colonial lanterns on short poles, with cobra heads on arms over the busier streets. The data's style codes come without a key, so matching them to the styles in Dominion's catalogue is ours. The water in the Hague is where the City's GIS maps open water (its Water layer); the layer gives no names, and the bank's height, about a metre above the water, is ours. People keep to the sidewalks and cross at corners: the sidewalks are what the parcels leave between them and the street, and where the crosswalks go is ours. Places of worship, the public-safety building and the sanitation district's facility are left as they are.
