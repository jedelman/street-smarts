street-smarts

A Christopher Alexander provocation engine for neighborhood imagination. v0.1.

The Military Circle site in Norfolk, VA, run through Alexander's pattern language: streets, squares, buildings, shops, trees and a light rail stop, all generated. The walk on the front page lets you fly through it as a sparrow; this is the long read: what it would cost and who would pay, how it got here, and the chorus of opinions on it.

Real ground

Military Circle, Norfolk: the old mall site's 25 City GIS parcels, with today's neighbors from Overture Maps around the edge.

Generated, not drawn

Every street, square, building, shop door and tree, and the light rail stop, comes out of the pattern pipeline, regenerated on every deploy.

Ask it

Touch anything and it names the pattern that placed it. Eavesdrop on the people and they'll tell you what the chorus of opinions below thinks of it, good and bad. The people, benches and pigeons are ours.

What it would cost, and who would pay

This is public finance before it's retail: about $1.8B of building on land the public already owns. The plan's own measurements, taken on every deploy, run through sourced figures and stated assumptions. A sense of scale, not a pro forma.

$1.8Bto build: homes $990M, workplaces $590M, shops $240M, and the land at what the public paid for it, $13M. Before the streets and utilities.
4 or 5phases of about $370M to $460M each (our guess). Norfolk's housing authority puts the next stage of St. Paul's, Young Terrace and Calvert Square, at $1 billion, in phases from 2027.

1.7%the interest rate at which that pays the whole cost back in 30 years. Norfolk itself borrows at up to 4.1%; Virginia Housing lends at about 6.2%.

$17Ma year in property tax, assessed at what the buildings cost. The land stays the public's under ground leases, as at St. Paul's.

How Norfolk paid for St. Paul's

The nearest thing Norfolk has done is the St. Paul's Area Transformation. Tidewater Gardens is being rebuilt as Kindred: 714 homes, in phases since 2022, and each phase is its own deal. Here is how one building in the fourth phase, 101 homes costing $50.9M (about $504,000 a home), is paid for:

The city's part sits mostly outside the stack. It builds the new streets and utilities, $60.6M across the phases, and counts them as an in-kind contribution. The land stays the housing authority's: it's ground-leased to each phase, with an option to buy the building back after 15 years. The homes stay affordable for 30 to 50 years. And Norfolk borrows cheaply: its 2025 bonds, rated AAA by S&P, sold at yields of 2.3 to 4.1%.

It hasn't been quick or cheap. Virginia Housing flagged that building's cost a home as over its limit ($501,470 against $331,194). The new streets went from about $26M to $61M. The 2022 promise of completion by 2025 now runs to 2028. And the housing authority counts 101 of 618 Tidewater Gardens families back so far.

The same shape, here

The city already owns most of this site, as the housing authority owns St. Paul's. Say it keeps the land, leases it to each phase and builds the streets, and the money comes the way St. Paul's money did. Grants and tax credits cost the project nothing to carry. The city lends at its own rate. A first mortgage covers the rest. Move the shares:

Tax credits only pay for homes kept affordable, which then rent for less, and grants are competitive. The shops are priced at $27 a square foot before running costs, a developer's return. That's more than a stack like this costs to carry on them, and the difference is part of what's left for the city. The homes and offices earn today's Norfolk rents.

What's in it

3.2Msquare feet of floor space, about 3.5× the old mall's 935,000 (54% occupied in 2020)
2,300homes, on the floors P9 Scattered Work gives to living: about 5,300 people. All of downtown Norfolk had 4,691 in 2023.
9,400desks, on the floors P9 gives to work: offices, studios and workshops, and a lunch crowd for the shops below
$96Ma year the homes and desks would spend in shops, if all of it stayed here: $38M from homes, $59M from desks
740shop fronts along 4.1 km of street, 699 of them P87's own small doors
2,023trees, 137 of them bald cypress, in a meadow with 8 ponds and 3.1 hectares of wetland, the rain kept above ground (P64 Pools and Streams). No parking lots.

Back to the aquifer. The homes and desks would send about 0.7 million gallons of wastewater a day to HRSD, at Virginia's design flows. HRSD's SWIFT program treats wastewater to drinking-water standard and pumps it deep underground into the Potomac Aquifer, to replenish it and slow the land sinking. Each well at its new James River plant takes up to 2 million gallons a day. Treated that way, this site's flow would keep about 35% of one well busy. The rain is another matter: the ponds can soak it into shallow ground, but nothing goes down a SWIFT well that isn't clean enough to drink. A conversation starter, not a design: which plant would treat this site's flow isn't modeled, and the two full-scale SWIFT plants are in Newport News and Suffolk.

How many ground floors can the people upstairs carry?

740 shop fronts for about 5,300 people is about 140 for every 1,000. Paris has 28 ground-floor shops, cafés and services per 1,000 residents, Barcelona 37, and both have tourists and commuters helping. By the block model's own measure, the people upstairs carry about 150 to 200 ground floors, and most of those aren't shops. Of Barcelona's 61,875 ground-floor businesses, 21,167 are shops and 33,740 are services: hairdressers, clinics, agencies, repair, offices at street level. At Barcelona's rates, the people here would carry about 70 shops and 110 services. The rest of the 740 fronts are the bet: the people who come to work here, and pull from the light rail and the region.

What a shop here has to sell

A shop's rent can be priced three ways, and each answers a different question.

What the market asks
Landlords price space by what similar space nearby rents for. Norfolk's average ask is $14.75 a square foot a year before running costs, and that's mostly older buildings: new shop space in Hampton Roads commonly starts at $35, often more (Old Dominion University's 2026 market review). Nothing like this block has been built here, so there's nothing to compare it with.
What a developer needs
A developer builds when the rent, less running costs, returns enough on everything the building cost. That return has to beat what a buyer gets on a finished shopping center, 7.08% on average last quarter, by 1 to 1.5 points, or the developer would rather buy one. That's the yield on cost. At 8.33% of what this space costs, the rent comes to about $27 before running costs.
What it costs
Cost rent pays back what the space cost, with interest, over a loan's term, plus its running costs, and nothing more. Each year that's the cost × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−years). Austria's limited-profit housing associations set rents this way by law, for homes and shops alike: “no higher, but also no lower” than their costs, with the return on their own money capped at 3.5%. Their homes rent for about €6.50 a square meter a month, against €10.70 in private buildings built since 1945. Paris does something like it for shops: the city lends SEM Paris Commerces the money to buy ground floors, which it rents to independent shops below market, nearly 500 of them today. One bookshop pays about 40% under the market.

We price this space at what it costs. Building it costs $205 to $290 a square foot (strip retail in Charlotte, the nearest city in Rider Levett Bucknall's survey; we use the middle, $248). Add 30% for design, permits, fees and financing. Add the land at what the public paid for it, $13.4M in 2020, which is about $3 a square foot of the plan's floors. That comes to about $325 a square foot, before the site's own streets, utilities and landscaping, which the survey leaves out. Who carries the money sets the rent:

At a developer's return or a bank's rate, new space here needs about $27 to $28 before running costs: nearly double Norfolk's average ask, and still under what new space in the region leases for. Public money brings it to about $19. Held in common and paid for, the rent is only the running costs, and a shop's break-even falls by about 25% against public money. The break-evens are floors: the shopkeeper earns Norfolk's living wage, and utilities, insurance and card fees aren't counted.

The pull gap

If every dollar the people upstairs spend in shops stayed here, about $38M a year, it would carry about 160 shops at that break-even. The desks bring a lunch crowd: about 9,400 of them, spending about $59M a year near the office, more than the households. Together, about $96M, enough for about 400. That counts every desk filled five days a week, which hybrid work isn't, and prices the desks' spending in 2011 dollars, which understates it. The other 340 fronts need about $80M a year from outside the site, roughly 1.5× everything downtown's Granby sub-district sold in FY2023. That's on purpose: the plan is a frame for a regional destination, and the gap is the pull it would have to earn.

Against the doom loop

Cushman & Wakefield and Christopher Leinberger studied 208 walkable urban places in 15 US cities, which they call WalkUPs: 3% of those cities' land, 26% of their real estate value, 37% of their tax revenue. The ones that do best mix living, working and playing; downtowns lost value because they were mostly work. Their best mix, by floor area, averages 42% work (anywhere from 24% to 62% can work), 32% living and 26% play. And WalkUPs, they write, are not “bedroom communities, where residential tends to be between 80% to 90% of all inventory.”

With homes over shops and nothing else, this plan was 77% living. Now Alexander's P9 Scattered Work gives every floor a use and scatters work through the homes, a floor at a time across every building: “Prohibit large concentrations of work without family life around them. Prohibit large concentrations of family life without workplaces around them.” It comes to 54% living, 30% work and 16% play:

The mall was the doom loop's suburban cousin: one use, and when the shopping left, nothing held the place up. Homes over shops alone would have traded it for another monoculture, with shops waiting all day for people to come home. Work on every block changes that: offices, studios and workshops bring a lunch crowd, about 9,400 desks whose spending nearby would outrun the households', and the shops get a day as well as an evening. In the study, 70% of these places' foot traffic came from people visiting things to do. That's the pull the gap above needs, and shops are only part of it: a market hall, music, a hotel by the station. Some of what the people here need takes more of them, too: a walkable grocery store wants 5,000 to 10,000 homes nearby before it opens, and the plan has about 2,300.

Their other levers, and what each looks like held in common. More homes for sale, not just for rent: here, limited-equity co-ops and land-trust homes, which keep the gains with the people who live there. Place management, which they mean as business improvement districts: here, the neighborhood running its own streets and squares. Faster permits and incentives for reuse: the city's call. And the tax base: at Norfolk's rate, the plan's buildings would pay about $11M a year in real estate tax if assessed at what they cost to build, before counting the land.

The light rail

HRT studied taking the Tide two miles from Newtown Road, past Sentara Leigh, to a last station on this site (an early estimate put it near $225M), then paused the study in August 2023 because the city hadn't settled where that station goes. It was still on hold in July 2025. The plan sketches an answer: a platform 133 m from its streets, at the end of a line from the direction of Newtown Road. It isn't HRT's route. Today the nearest station, Military Highway, is about 1 km away, across I‑264.

How the numbers are built
  • Measured from the plan on every deploy (tools/vibe-render/plan_numbers.py): floor area is each building's footprint times its floors; upper floors are that minus the ground floor; shop fronts are the ones the render opens at street-facing doors.
  • Homes: the floors given to living × 85% usable ÷ 900 sq ft a home, our assumption (range: 80% and 1,000 sq ft, to 88% and 800). Every floor of offices means fewer homes and a bigger lunch crowd. People: 2.27 a household, Norfolk's average.
  • Ground floors per resident: Paris, 60,846 ground-floor shops, bars, restaurants and services, 28 per 1,000 residents (Apur's 2023 census); Barcelona, 61,875 ground-floor businesses, 3.7 per 100 (2024), of which 21,167 are shops and 33,740 services; its residents (about 1.67 million) are implied by those two figures. City-wide figures, with visitors' and commuters' spending in them, so a ceiling for what residents alone carry; Barcelona's census credits its density to “urban compactness and the large number of visitors.”
  • What it earns: the homes at Norfolk's average effective rent, $1.80 a square foot a month ($1,598 a unit; Cushman & Wakefield | Thalhimer, Q3 2026; Class A across Hampton Roads asks $2,110, Northmarq), less 5% vacancy and 28% running costs, property tax included (BAE's Seattle assumptions; Norfolk's vacancy is 3.1%). The offices at Norfolk's Class A full-service asking rent, $26.78 (Thalhimer, Q3 2026), less 10% vacancy (ours; Norfolk's is 14.1%) and 32% running costs (BAE), before fitting them out ($40–50 a square foot, ODU). The shops at $27 before running costs, less 5% vacancy (ours). What buyers pay for finished buildings: apartments 5.6%, offices 7.3–7.5% (MSCI Real Capital Analytics, February 2026), Hampton Roads offices 7.7% (ODU), multi-tenant retail 7.08% (Northmarq).
  • The stack: grants and tax credits carry nothing; the city's share of the borrowing is at its 2025 bond yield, 4.11%; the rest is a first mortgage at 6.18%, Virginia Housing's rate in the St. Paul's building above (its rate sheet showed 6.154% on November 25, 2026); both are paid off over 30 years (our term). Phases, tax-exempt bond limits, construction loans and lease-up aren't modeled. The phases are four or five, our guess, equal in cost.
  • St. Paul's: the building is Tidewater Gardens B-2 at Kindred, from its 2025 application for 4% tax credits to Virginia Housing: $50,939,077 in all, with $25.72M of tax-exempt bonds during construction. The sources above are its permanent ones; the shares are of the total, and $0.4M is unaccounted for in the application's own list. The developer is Brinshore; the master developer for Young Terrace and Calvert Square is Gilbane. The city's streets and utilities ($60,575,654.71) are from the city manager's letter in the application; the streets' cost rise and delay from the city council's October 2025 acceptance of federal BUILD money; the cost limit from Virginia Housing's own form; the return count from the housing authority's October 2026 board packet. Norfolk's 2025 bonds: $241.3M tax-exempt and $14.8M taxable, yields 2.320–4.110%.
  • What the space costs:
    • Building it: the middle of RLB's $205–290 a square foot for strip retail in Charlotte (Q3 2026). That's hard construction cost only: RLB's definition leaves out site work (utilities, paving, landscaping), furniture and fittings, and every soft cost, from land to design, permits and financing (its 2021 Riders Digest; the 2026 report says only “hard construction costs”).
    • Soft costs: 30% on top, ours, inside the published range. In Seattle, BAE puts design, legal and accounting alone at 18–20% of hard costs, with a 4% developer fee and construction financing on top (2025). In California, the Terner Center's budgets put fees, financing, consulting and the developer's fee at 37–40% (2023; our arithmetic from its figures). Brookings has soft costs at 20–30% of total cost for a straightforward apartment project (2020).
    • Land: what the public paid. Norfolk's Economic Development Authority bought the mall and its outparcels for $11M and the hotel for $2.4M in 2020, and sold the mall to the city for $10 in 2025. Spread over the plan's floors, that's the per-foot figure above. The city doesn't hold the whole site: Sentara owns the old J.C. Penney building and much of the parking inside the ring road.
    • Running it: $10.33 a square foot a year, IREM's 2024 national figure for offices, taxes included. No public retail figure exists, and IREM has stopped publishing the series.
  • Rent four ways: a developer earns 8.33% a year on the whole cost: Northmarq's Q3 2026 average cap rate for multi-tenant retail, 7.08% (sales over $2.5M; Southeast 6.92%, Mid-Atlantic 7.82%), plus 1.25 points, the middle of the 1–1.5 point spread the Terner Center heard from developers and BAE uses in Seattle. A bank loan is the prime rate, 7.00%, over 25 years (our term); public money is Norfolk's own borrowing: its 2025 general obligation bonds (rated AAA by S&P, AA+ by Fitch) sold at yields up to 4.110% for a 2045 maturity, amortized here over 30 years (our term). The prime rate is from the Federal Reserve's H.15 for November 24, 2026, when the 10-year Treasury was 4.91%: tax-exempt city bonds cost less than the federal government's. Already paid for is running costs only. The market's asks: $14.75 triple-net for Norfolk and $16.96 for Hampton Roads (Cushman & Wakefield | Thalhimer, Q3 2026); new construction “commonly commenced at $35 psf on a triple-net basis” (ODU, 2026).
  • Cost rent elsewhere: Austria's Limited-Profit Housing Act sets rent at what covers the buildings' costs and reserves, “nicht höher, aber auch nicht niedriger”, for homes and business premises (§13). It counts construction, land and financing in the cost, and caps interest on the association's own money at 3.5%, 5% at most (§14). The rents are Austria-wide averages for homes, before running costs (Statistik Austria, Q2 2026). Paris: SEM Paris Commerces (formerly Semaest) has worked on more than 800 premises since 2004 and manages nearly 500. The city lends it the money to buy, and subsidizes it to offset “des loyers inférieurs au prix de marché” (rents below market; Ville de Paris, 2025). The bookshop at about 40% under the market is the city's own example (2023). That's subsidized rent, not cost rent in law.
  • A shop's break-even: rent for 900 sq ft plus Norfolk's living wage for one adult ($47,273, MIT) divided by a 31.1% gross margin, the average for all US retail in 2022 (Census). Margins run from 28% at grocers and 31% at wine shops to 45% at book and hobby stores and 51% at clothing stores. Cafés and restaurants keep about 68% after food and drink, but pay staff: this doesn't model them.
  • Desks: the floors given to work × 85% usable ÷ 12 m² (129 sq ft) a desk, the Employment Density Guide's figure for professional-services offices (UK Homes & Communities Agency, 2015; office gross runs 15–20% over net). That's a planned office. US offices hold about three times the space a worker: EIA counts 507 sq ft of building, lobbies, halls and empty space included, per office worker on the main shift (2018). What a desk spends nearby: $129.18 a workweek on goods, services and food around the office, less transport and online shopping (ICSC's survey of 4,000 office workers, October 2011; in 2011 dollars, not adjusted), for 48 weeks a year (ours). Every desk filled five days a week: hybrid work would cut it, and some desks are the people upstairs, counted twice. The walk's Flows draws the same model.
  • Spending: $15,662 a household a year on food at home and out, clothes, alcohol, furnishings, housekeeping, personal care, reading and a quarter of entertainment (BLS, 2023, middle income fifth where published).
  • Live, work, play: each floor's use is P9 Scattered Work's, from the pipeline: ground floors with shop doors are play (shop fronts take about 88% of them; entrances and common rooms the rest), ground floors without them are workshops, and upper floors are homes with work added a floor at a time until it reaches 30% of the floor area, a share inside the report's band. The report's shares are of real estate inventory by square footage. Its optimal mix, found by a random-forest model balancing place-based GDP and value per square foot, averages 42.0% work (90% band 23.8–61.7%), 31.8% living (22.8–46.6%) and 26.2% play (17.0–31.0%); downtowns are 69.7% work, 15.9% living and 14.5% play. It sets aside suburban WalkUPs, “redevelopment of edge cities” among them, which is what this site would be. The grocery threshold is from the same report.
  • Tax: Norfolk's real estate rate, $1.23 per $100 of assessed value, on the middle of RLB's Charlotte hard costs: $248 a square foot for shops (strip retail, $205–290), $298 for homes (multifamily, $220–375) and $318 for work (secondary offices, $265–370). Assessments needn't match cost; land isn't counted.
  • Water: P64 Pools and Streams puts ponds where the open ground has room, each in a ring of marsh, bald cypress in the shallows, streams along the fewest links that join them, and a boardwalk out to each pond a street is near enough to: across the marsh, then along the shallows to a platform. The pattern asks for paths and footbridges along the water; the boardwalks take the shape of the Bald Cypress Trail at First Landing State Park, whose trail guide promises “boardwalk scenic swamp overviews” and turtles “sunbathing on logs in the swamps.” The park is a National Natural Landmark as “the northernmost location on the East Coast where subtropical and temperate plants grow and thrive together.” In the walk, the swamp's dark water, Spanish moss, turtles, herons and egrets are ours, after it. There was no natural pond to keep (the site was a mall and its parking), so this is a stormwater wetland: the pattern's own call to keep rainwater above ground. No grading, depth or drainage is modeled; the site's levels aren't in the data.
  • Wastewater and SWIFT: 100 gallons a day a resident and 15 a desk, Virginia's design flows for new sewage works (9VAC25-790-460, Table 3: dwellings, per person, which already allows for a little infiltration and small businesses; offices aren't listed, so the low end of factories, 15–35 per person per 8-hour shift). Design flows run high on purpose, so this is a ceiling. SWIFT: HRSD's James River plant will recharge the Potomac Aquifer with up to 16 million gallons a day, up to two million per well, for $500–600 million, under construction since spring 2022 (HRSD); its Research Center in Suffolk already puts a million gallons a day back. HRSD serves 1.9 million people in 20 cities and counties. USGS describes the recharge as a way to combat land subsidence.
  • Heights: El Born's, not Alexander's: the pipeline caps ordinary buildings at six floors (P21's own default is four, and the P21 opinion marks the plan down for it), with a few widely spaced exceptions at eight.
  • Not modeled: visitors, competition from nearby stores, office attendance below five days a week, the site's own streets, utilities and landscaping, and the shopkeeper living upstairs at cost too, which would lower the pay a shop has to cover.
  • The acreage disagrees: the site's perimeter in the data measures 69 acres. The city's site page and its 2020 announcement of the RFQ say about 74 for the mall, its parking and outparcels. The RFQ itself says about 89 with parking and outparcels, and the project page says 97.7. Nothing here is per acre.

    Vision

    Everything above this line is computed: real parcels, real pattern math, real citations, honestly labeled gaps. This section is the opposite, on purpose — a place for imagination to run ahead of what the pipeline has actually verified. A mood board, a hand sketch, an AI-illustrated concept of what this could feel like to stand in — dreaming out loud about a place is real, necessary work, especially for a neighborhood that's been told "no" by real systems for a long time. It isn't a lesser kind of contribution than the geometry above.

    It will never be merged into the render above, and the render above will never quietly borrow from it. Nothing here is a proposal, a commitment, or a claim about this site — it's a place to imagine together before anything is decided, kept in view, but never confused with what's actually real.

    Nothing has been added here yet. This space is reserved for the neighborhood's own vision — sketches, references, AI-illustrated concepts — each one clearly labeled, none of them mistaken for a real proposal.

    This tool does not measure your neighborhood. Aliveness is what Alexander called the quality without a name — by definition not fully namable. What you're about to read is a chorus of opinions, each citing its source, encoded in math. The interesting part is where they disagree, or where they tell you what they couldn't see.

    What's accurate is the record of what people decide. Everything below is one or another opinion. The geometric voices come from Alexander & Salingaros. The activist voice comes from Jason and the Eastside Commons coalition. Your voice — the one not yet captured here — is the only one that knows whether this place is alive to live in.

    The map is one more opinion. Parcels are an administrative cut by Norfolk City GIS — they are not buildings, sidewalks, or the way people walk this place. A neighborhood is more than its polygons. Basemap tiles fetched from openstreetmap.org; if those don't load the parcels still work.
